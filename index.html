Seeing a speck is different
from seeing its detail.

Bring the two points closer. Their blurred images overlap until they are difficult to distinguish. Resolution describes the smallest separation at which two details can still be seen as separate.

Why is this an approximate limit?

Eyesight, illumination, contrast and viewing distance all matter. The chapter uses 100 µm at 25 cm as a useful reference. It does not mean that every object smaller than 100 µm is invisible in every situation.

03 WAYS OF SEEING PA2 EXAM FOCUS

Light has more
than one trick.

The same cell can look almost invisible, sharply outlined or brilliantly fluorescent. The difference comes from how the microscope turns light into information.

Magnification

How much larger the image appears. It helps your eye inspect the detail the instrument has captured.

Resolution

How close two details can be and still appear separate. A smaller resolvable distance means finer detail.

Contrast

How clearly a feature stands out against its background. A large, well-resolved image can still be hard to interpret if contrast is weak.

01   Refraction

Light changes direction at glass surfaces because its speed changes. A convex lens brings a parallel beam toward a focus; an object beyond that focus can produce a real image.

02   A real intermediate image

The objective forms A′B′ where actual rays cross. This image is enlarged and inverted. A screen placed at that plane could receive it.

The eyepiece receives A′B′ inside its focal length. Emerging rays diverge; their backward extensions meet at A″B″. No light actually travels along those dashed extensions.

See the equations and sign convention

Light travels left to right. A real object on a lens’s left has a positive object distance d. A real image on its right has positive v; a virtual image on its left has negative v. Both convex focal lengths are positive.

1/f = 1/d + 1/v   ·   v = fd/(d − f)
m = −v/d   ·   himage = mhobject

The objective is at x = 0. The eyepiece is at x = v₁ + dₑ. The final image is at x = v₁ + dₑ + v₂. A negative total image-height ratio means the final arrow points down.

Two different uses of “magnification”: this bench reports a linear image-height ratio. The microscope’s rated visual magnification is objective magnification × eyepiece angular magnification. Do not treat the bench’s focal lengths as the × numbers printed on a microscope.

    07 MEASURE THE INVISIBLE EXPLORE BEYOND PA2

    Bigger is only
    half the story.

    A microscope helps you see. A measurement tells you how much. Choose the right model, keep the units consistent, and ask whether the extra enlargement reveals anything new.

    Two lens systems. One product.

    M = objective power × eyepiece power
    400×

    This is nominal viewing magnification. It does not, by itself, calibrate a photo resized on a screen. A 100× objective often requires immersion oil and the correct technique.

    From an image to a real size.

    Actual size = image size ÷ linear magnification

    Use a scale-calibrated image at its stated display or print size. Enlarging the photo changes its linear magnification.

    Estimate a cell’s span.

    Average cell span ≈ field diameter ÷ number of cells

    Assume similar-sized cells fill the diameter with negligible gaps. This is an estimate, not a precision measurement of every cell.

    A closer view. A smaller field.

    D₂ = D₁ × M₁ ÷ M₂

    Assume the same viewing system, with field diameter inversely proportional to magnification. Changing cameras, eyepiece field number or crop can change this relationship.

    Why do the ray lab’s magnifications look different?

    The ray lab reports signed linear image-height ratios for two ideal thin lenses. The familiar objective × eyepiece calculation combines the instrument’s rated objective magnification with its eyepiece viewing power. They describe related but different measurement conventions.

    MAGNIFICATION ≠ RESOLUTION

    A bigger blur is
    still a blur.

    Try increasing display enlargement while keeping resolution fixed. The picture becomes bigger, but missing detail does not return. This is empty magnification.

    Now improve resolution by decreasing the minimum resolvable separation. The same two specimen points can become distinguishable. Resolving power increases as this distance decreases; it can be expressed as its reciprocal.

    ~100 µmHuman-eye reference
    ~0.2 µmConventional light
    ~0.2 nmAdvanced electron example

    Approximate benchmarks, not promises for every specimen or instrument. Conventional light performance depends on wavelength and numerical aperture; advanced electron instruments require demanding conditions.

    A sharp 100× image becomes blurred at 400×. What should you check?

    Check fine focus, illumination, the objective and slide first. Blur alone does not prove empty magnification. Use that term when additional enlargement reveals no new resolved detail even with the image properly focused.

    08 BEYOND VISIBLE LIGHT Explore beyond PA2

    A different beam.
    A deeper view.

    Some questions are too small for a school light microscope. Electron microscopes use a much shorter wavelength—and ask much more of the specimen.

    Let electrons pass through.

    In a transmission electron microscope, electrons pass through an extremely thin specimen. Differences in transmission and scattering create contrast, revealing internal ultrastructure in a two-dimensional image.

    50–90 nmtypical textbook section
    0.1–0.2 nmpossible instrument resolution*

    *A representative high-performance range under suitable conditions. It is not a promise of atomic detail in every biological preparation.

    The detector records a signal at each scanned position. Those values build the image.

    60 kV200 kV
    0.00418 nmelectron wavelength

    Visible green light is about 550 nm. A shorter electron wavelength helps make finer detail possible; achieved resolution also depends on the instrument and specimen.

    What does this number mean?

    The calculation uses the relativistic de Broglie relation for an electron accelerated through the selected voltage. Approximately 0.005 nm is one example, not a fixed wavelength of every electron microscope.

    λ = h / √[2mₑeV(1 + eV / 2mₑc²)]. Here V is in volts; the result is converted to nanometres. The diagram remains schematic and does not simulate a real electron column.

    Prepare the specimen for the question.

    For conventional biological TEM, specimens are commonly fixed, dehydrated, embedded and cut into ultrathin sections with a glass or diamond knife. Electron-dense stains can improve contrast by scattering electrons.

    A vacuum, a beam, magnetic lenses.

    Electrons scatter in air, so electron optics operate in a vacuum. Electromagnetic lens systems guide and focus the beam. A heated tungsten filament is one possible electron source; field-emission sources are another.

    Conventional TEM and SEM do not show cells living naturally. Preparation, vacuum conditions and electron exposure change what can be observed.

    Why are electron microscopes usually found in research centres?

    They need costly equipment, skilled operation, specialised specimen preparation, vacuum systems and careful maintenance. Stable electrical, mechanical and environmental conditions also matter. The useful extra detail comes with practical trade-offs.

    §9.6.1 · Digital microscopy

    From one eye
    to a whole classroom.

    A camera can turn the optical image into pixels and send it to a screen. Students can discuss the same field of view, record a time sequence, or compare observations.

    A larger screen or digital zoom does not, by itself, improve the microscope’s optical resolution.

    Camera mode: the image is converted to pixels and displayed for everyone. The same resolved structures are enlarged on the screen.

    §9.6.1 · Super-resolution

    Work around
    the conventional limit.

    Ordinary light microscopy blurs very close points together. Some specialised fluorescence methods extract finer spatial information using controlled illumination or the positions of individual emitters.

    Two points. One blurred impression.

    Two nearby fluorescent emitters produce overlapping diffraction patterns. Enlarging this image cannot separate information that the system has not resolved.

    Detail has practical limits.

    Conventional light resolution is around 0.2 µm under favourable conditions. Electron microscopy can reveal much finer structure but needs suitable samples, vacuum and specialist preparation. Digital enlargement cannot replace missing detail.

    01 What is a microscope?

    An instrument for examining specimens or structures too small for unaided detail. It can reveal biological material such as cells and microorganisms, and nonliving material such as dust or microelectronic components. “Micro” refers to small; the word’s roots refer to viewing. Return to §9.1 →

    02 Which microscope should I use?

    Start with the question: a living process, internal ultrastructure or surface shape? Instrument choice depends on specimen size, resolution, contrast and preparation—not just the largest advertised magnification. Compare light, TEM and SEM →

    03 Magnification

    The factor by which an image is enlarged. Bigger does not automatically mean more detail. For the instrument’s nominal viewing power, multiply objective × eyepiece. For a calibrated image, actual size = image size ÷ linear magnification. These conventions must not be confused.

    04 Resolution

    The minimum separation at which two points can be distinguished. Smaller is better. The chapter’s approximate human-eye reference is 100 µm at 25 cm. Conventional light microscopy can reach about 0.2 µm under suitable conditions; some advanced electron instruments resolve much smaller distances. Try the two-point lab →

    05 Light microscopy

    Visible light and glass lens systems form an image. Illumination and specimen contrast matter as well as lens power. A compound microscope’s objective forms the intermediate image; the eyepiece magnifies it for viewing.

    06 Refraction and the two images

    Refraction bends light at boundaries where its speed changes. In the compound-microscope model, an object just beyond the objective focus forms a real, inverted, enlarged image. Inside the eyepiece’s focal length, this produces a virtual image that remains inverted relative to the original specimen. Mirrors may redirect illumination; they are not the magnifying mechanism. Rebuild Figure 9.5 →

    07 Simple, compound and stereo

    A simple magnifier uses one converging lens. A compound microscope uses objective and eyepiece lens systems. A modern stereo microscope uses two optical pathways to provide depth perception. “Dissecting” is not a reliable synonym for “single lens.”

    08 Fluorescence

    Suitable excitation light causes fluorescent molecules to emit light at longer wavelengths. Filters select the light reaching the detector. Specific structures appear bright against a dark background; unlabelled structures need not appear.

    09 Phase contrast

    Phase differences caused by transparent structures become visible brightness differences. The method is useful for living, unstained cells. It changes contrast, not the specimen’s actual size. Switch between views →

    10 Temporary and permanent mounts

    A temporary wet mount is useful for short-term observations and living responses when the preparation preserves life. A conventional permanent preparation fixes and stains tissue, removes water, uses compatible clearing and mounting steps, and protects it for storage. Processing can introduce artefacts. Revisit Activities 9.4–9.5 →

    11 Electron beams and lenses

    Electrons behave as waves. At high accelerating voltages their wavelength is much shorter than visible light; the exact value depends on voltage. Electromagnetic fields focus the beam, and a vacuum limits interactions with gas molecules. The electron source may be a heated filament or a field-emission source.

    12 How TEM and SEM form images

    TEM forms an image using electrons transmitted through an ultrathin specimen. SEM scans a focused beam and detects signals such as secondary and backscattered electrons. Calling SEM only “reflection” misses the signal-generation process.

    13 Internal details or surfaces?

    TEM reveals internal ultrastructure in thin sections and forms a two-dimensional projection. SEM gives surface images with a three-dimensional appearance; a standard SEM image is not automatically a measured 3D model. Ordinary biological EM preparation cannot preserve an ongoing living response.

    14 Applications and new possibilities

    Microscopy supports pathology, biological research, materials and chip inspection, forensic comparisons and environmental studies. Digital capture shares a view; super-resolution methods use specialised optical and computational methods to go beyond conventional fluorescence resolution. Match every conclusion to what the evidence can establish.
